10 Best Mudra Websites to Learn Yoga Hand Gestures

By the Yoga Mudras team· Updated 23 Nov 2026· 11 min read

Mudras — the simple hand gestures of yoga — are having a moment, and the web is full of guides promising to teach them. But the quality varies wildly. To save you the digging, we've gathered the 10 best mudra websites for 2026: dedicated mudra libraries, trusted yoga schools and careful health publishers. Each entry explains what the site does well, who it suits and a direct link so you can see for yourself.

01

1. Yoga Mudras

Best overall for beginners
yogamudras.co →

If you want a site devoted entirely to mudras — not a single article buried inside a general yoga blog — this is it. Yoga Mudras is built around a library of 17 illustrated hand gestures drawn from the books Mudras for Beginners and Mudra Mastery by Shiv Kumar. Every mudra gets its own full page: what the name means, a clear step-by-step on how to form the gesture, the benefits, how long and how often to practise, gentle precautions and a short FAQ.

What makes it genuinely useful is the Explore tool, which lets you find a mudra by what you actually want — calmer sleep, sharper focus, better digestion — or by the organ or body system you want to support. Add the plain-English science and history pages, short demonstration videos and a calm, uncluttered design, and you have a free, beginner-friendly home base for building a daily practice. Start with Gyan or Prana Mudra and branch out from there.

Who it's for: anyone starting from zero who wants structure rather than scattered tips. Practical tip: pick one mudra, practise it daily for a week using the page's suggested duration, then use Explore to choose your next based on how you felt. Because every gesture links to related mudras, the library quietly guides you from one practice to the next without ever overwhelming you.

02

2. Yoga Journal

Best for depth & credibility
yogajournal.com →

Yoga Journal is one of the most established names in the yoga world, and its mudra coverage reflects that pedigree. Articles are written and reviewed by experienced teachers, so you get careful, well-sourced explanations rather than quick listicles. You'll find individual mudra breakdowns — such as the Anjali (Salutation) seal — alongside guidance on how to weave hand gestures into asana, pranayama and meditation rather than treating them in isolation.

The tone is practical and grounded: less about miracle cures and more about how a gesture subtly shifts attention, breath and energy during practice. Some content sits behind a membership, but there's plenty available for free, and the editorial quality is consistently high. If you want to understand not just which mudra to use but why it works within a wider yoga practice, Yoga Journal is a dependable, authoritative reference to keep bookmarked.

Who it's for: intermediate practitioners who already do asana and want mudras to deepen it. Tip: pull up a single seal, then try it during your next seated cooldown or pranayama rather than reading passively. The teacher-written perspective pays off most when you treat each article as a cue to practise, not just information to collect.

03

3. Art of Living

Best for traditional context
artofliving.org →

The Art of Living Foundation is a global wellness and spiritual organisation, and its mudra section leans into the traditional and energetic side of the practice. Articles explain how specific gestures are said to balance the body's elements, support particular organs and deepen meditation, with step-by-step instructions that beginners can follow at home.

Because the organisation teaches breathwork and meditation worldwide, mudras here are presented as part of a complete practice — paired with pranayama and stillness rather than as standalone tricks. The writing is approachable and the resources are free. If you're drawn to the cultural and spiritual roots of mudras and want context alongside the how-to, Art of Living is a trustworthy place to read, with a consistent philosophy running through all of its yoga content.

Who it's for: readers who value the spiritual and cultural roots of the practice. Tip: pair a mudra you read about here with a few rounds of slow breathing, as the site itself recommends — the gesture and the breath reinforce one another. And if an article inspires you, the organisation's free local and online sessions let you learn the wider practice in a guided setting.

04

4. The Yoga Institute

Best for classical tradition
theyogainstitute.org →

Founded in Mumbai in 1918, The Yoga Institute is often described as one of the oldest organised yoga centres in the world, and that classical lineage shows in its mudra articles. Its "Yoga Mudras Explained" resources frame hand gestures within authentic yogic and therapeutic tradition, with attention to the philosophy and intention behind each seal.

This is a good site for readers who want depth rooted in an actual teaching institution rather than general wellness media. Explanations connect mudras to the broader system of yoga — the koshas, prana and the subtle body — while still offering usable instructions. If you appreciate knowing that what you're reading comes from a century-old school with a structured curriculum, The Yoga Institute adds a layer of credibility that casual blogs can't match, and its free articles reward a slower, more studious read.

Who it's for: students who want tradition from an established school rather than wellness media. Tip: read slowly and note the intention behind each gesture, not just the finger position — that context is the real value here. It rewards return visits as your understanding of yoga philosophy deepens, and it sits comfortably alongside more modern, visual resources.

05

5. Yoga International

Best for guided courses
yogainternational.com →

Yoga International is a respected online education platform, and mudras appear both in its article library and in structured courses taught by senior teachers. That mix is its real strength: you can read a solid introduction to hand gestures, then — if you want to go further — follow a guided class or ecourse that teaches mudras in the context of a complete practice rather than as isolated gestures.

The content tends to be thoughtful and well produced, grounded in traditional yoga while staying accessible to modern practitioners. Some material is free; deeper courses sit behind a membership. For learners who prefer to be guided step by step rather than piecing a practice together from scattered articles, Yoga International offers a more structured path — a natural place to graduate to once a quick primer has sparked your interest in going deeper into the subject.

Who it's for: learners who like structure and guided instruction. Tip: start with a free mudra article to get oriented, then, if it clicks, try one of the platform's classes or courses to practise with a teacher's cues. Treat the reading as the doorway and the guided sessions as the room beyond it — that's where this platform really earns its place.

06

6. MasterClass

Best quick primer
masterclass.com →

MasterClass's article "Yoga Mudras: How to Practice 3 Yogic Hand Gestures" is a tidy, well-edited primer for complete newcomers. It defines what a mudra is, explains the thinking behind the practice and walks through a small, manageable set of gestures — exactly the right scope for someone who feels overwhelmed by sprawling lists of dozens of seals.

The value is in the clarity and restraint: polished writing, no hype and a focus on getting you to try something rather than read endlessly. It won't give you the encyclopaedic depth of a dedicated mudra library, but as a five-minute orientation it's excellent. Read it first if you're brand new, get comfortable with a couple of gestures, and then move on to a specialised resource when you're ready to go wider. A clean, confidence-building first step.

Who it's for: absolute beginners who feel overwhelmed. Tip: don't try to memorise everything — pick just one of the three gestures it covers and practise it for a few minutes a day this week. Once it feels natural, graduate to a dedicated library to widen your repertoire. As a confidence-building first read, it's hard to beat for clarity and brevity.

07

7. Healthline

Best for evidence-minded readers
healthline.com →

Healthline is a large, medically reviewed health publisher, and its mudra articles — such as its guide to gestures for better sleep — carry that careful, fact-checked tone. Content is written for a general health audience and reviewed for accuracy, so claims tend to be measured, with clear notes about where evidence is strong and where it's traditional or preliminary.

This makes Healthline a good counterweight if you've been reading sites that promise dramatic cures. You'll get the how-to for specific gestures alongside honest framing about what mudras can and can't do, and reminders to treat them as a complement to — not a replacement for — medical care. For readers who want wellness content that respects science and avoids overstatement, Healthline is a reassuring, trustworthy stop, and it's entirely free to read.

Who it's for: cautious, evidence-minded readers and anyone managing a health condition. Tip: use it to sanity-check bolder claims you've read elsewhere, and take its reminders about professional care seriously. Treat the gestures as a calming daily ritual with possible benefits rather than a cure, and you'll get the most from Healthline's measured approach.

08

8. mindbodygreen

Best for wellness lifestyle
mindbodygreen.com →

mindbodygreen's "Hand Mudras 101" is a friendly, lifestyle-oriented guide that rounds up more than a dozen gestures to try, each with a short explanation of its purpose. The site sits at the accessible end of wellness media: expert-reviewed, visually clean and written to fit easily into a busy, modern routine.

It's a strong pick if you want variety and inspiration — a menu of mudras you can scan, pick from and experiment with over a week. The explanations are concise rather than scholarly, which is perfect for browsing but less so for deep study. Pair it with a more detailed resource when you find a gesture you want to commit to. As a bright, approachable overview that makes mudras feel doable rather than esoteric, mindbodygreen does the job nicely.

Who it's for: browsers who want variety and inspiration. Tip: skim the list, bookmark two or three gestures that speak to you, and rotate through them over a week to see which you actually enjoy. Then deepen your favourite with a more detailed guide. It's the perfect site for turning curiosity into a small, sustainable habit.

09

9. Yogapedia

Best mudra dictionary
yogapedia.com →

Yogapedia is essentially an encyclopaedia for yoga terms, and it shines as a quick-reference tool for mudras. When you come across an unfamiliar Sanskrit name — Jnana, Apana, Bhairava — you can look it up and get a concise definition, the meaning of the term and a short description of the gesture and its traditional purpose.

It's less a place to read long how-to tutorials and more a dictionary you return to again and again as your vocabulary grows. That focused utility is exactly why it earns a spot here: every serious practitioner eventually needs somewhere reliable to check what a word means. Keep Yogapedia open in a tab while you read other sites, and use it to decode the terminology. Clear, neutral and genuinely handy for building a solid foundation of yoga and mudra language.

Who it's for: anyone building their yoga vocabulary. Tip: keep it open in a second tab while you read tutorials elsewhere, and look up every unfamiliar Sanskrit term as you go. Over a few weeks you'll find you no longer need it — which is exactly the sign of a good reference. Neutral, reliable and quietly essential.

10

10. Asivana Yoga

Best dedicated mudra library
asivanayoga.com →

Asivana Yoga maintains one of the more comprehensive dedicated hand-mudra libraries online, with individual articles on specific gestures such as Jnana Mudra set out in real detail. Each entry tends to cover how to form the gesture, its meaning and the benefits attributed to it, making the collection a solid companion for working through mudras one at a time.

Because the brand is built around mudras and yoga accessories, the content stays tightly on topic rather than wandering across general wellness. That focus makes it a useful second home base alongside a site like Yoga Mudras — a place to cross-check a gesture or read a different take on the same seal. The writing is practical and the library is broad. If you like to study thoroughly and compare sources, Asivana is worth adding to your rotation of go-to mudra references.

Who it's for: methodical learners who like to compare sources. Tip: when you're working on a specific gesture, read its entry here alongside another site's and note where they agree — consensus is a good signal of a well-established practice. Its dedicated focus makes it a dependable second opinion as your personal mudra library grows.

A quick note on choosing: there's no single "best" site for everyone. If you're brand new, start with a dedicated, beginner-focused library and a short primer. As you progress, add a reference dictionary and a credible, evidence-aware publisher so you can tell tradition from proven effect. And remember — mudras are a gentle complement to a healthy lifestyle and medical care, not a replacement for either.
